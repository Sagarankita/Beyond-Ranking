1 Introduction
Assignment 8 asks us to treat optimization as a transferable problem-solving method. The
work therefore focuses on identifying a process, defining decision variables and measurable
objectives, applying suitable methods, checking correctness independently, and comparing
each result with a fair baseline. Tracks A–D exercise the optimization topics; Track E is the
selected process case study; Tracks F–J apply optimization to a compiler, operating system,
database, quantum computing and software engineering.
The project deliberately uses small benchmarks that can be rerun locally on a 16 GB
MacBook Air. Small instances make exact enumeration and invariant checks feasible; they
do not establish production scalability. The evidence distinguishes mathematical or query-
complexity statements from CPU measurements, simulator measurements and QEMU
measurements. Negative speedups, exhausted stopping budgets, equal model accuracy and
unresolved course-specific methods are retained as findings rather than edited away.
1.1 Objectives and workflow
For each experiment the workflow records the baseline, formal model, method choice, input
identity, constraints, repetitions, correctness checks and measured outcome. The shared
workflow used across tracks is shown below. Domain-specific implementations remain
separate even where they reuse helpers, so a result from one track is not counted as evidence
for another.

4.A Track A Continuous Optimization
4.A.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
A minimizes regularized binary logistic loss on 160 synthetic two-feature observations. Batch
gradient descent is the reference; eight first-order variants share the data, initialization, 60
passes, and three seeds. Newton and BFGS use the same logistic objective, while Gauss–
Newton is tested on a nonlinear least-squares exponential fit.
For labels yᵢ∈{0,1}, features xᵢ and parameter w, L(w)=n⁻¹Σ[log(1+eˣᵢᵀʷ)−yᵢxᵢᵀw]+(λ/2)||w||²,
with λ=0.1. Backpropagation computes ∇L; an optimizer separately changes w. The synthetic
convex objective, strongly convex quadratic, and tilted double-well landscape isolate
curvature and non-convex behavior.
L(w)=1/n Σᵢ[log(1+eˣᵢᵀʷ)−yᵢxᵢᵀw]+(λ/2)||w||² (1)
4.A.2 Algorithms and experimental method
First-order methods were batch GD, SGD, mini-batch SGD, Momentum, Nesterov, AdaGrad,
RMSProp and Adam. Curvature methods included Newton, Gauss–Newton, BFGS, exact
Hessian-vector products, diagonal Hessian, empirical Fisher and rank-1 approximation. A
small PyTorch tanh network demonstrates backward gradient computation before the
optimizer step. Central finite differences provide independent derivative checks.
4.A.3 Results and interpretation
Three-run first-order means ranged from 0.391126 (mini-batch) to 0.391433 (batch GD). All
first-order runs exhausted the 60-pass budget before the strict 10⁻⁶ tolerance. Newton and
BFGS reached about 0.391126; Gauss–Newton recovered planted parameters [1.5, −0.8]. The
logistic gradient check's maximum absolute error was 7.38×10⁻¹¹; the XOR model reached
100% training accuracy. On the tilted double well, the global minimum was near (−1.024, 0),
the local minimum near (0.974, 0), and the saddle near (0.0501, 0). Learning rate 0.22
diverged where the fixed-step bound was 0.2. Increasing λ reduced parameter norm
2.494→0.368 while accuracy changed 0.938→0.931; three noisy runs showed a nonzero
oscillating error floor.

4.B Track B Discrete Optimization
4.B.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
B maximizes value in a 0/1 selection problem subject to one capacity constraint. The study
uses three fixed knapsack instances with 3, 8 and 14 items, and compares density greedy,
binary ILP, branch-and-bound and dynamic programming.
Let xᵢ∈{0,1} indicate selection, vᵢ be value, wᵢ be weight and C be capacity. Maximize Σvᵢxᵢ
subject to Σwᵢxᵢ≤C. A separate weighted Max-Cut graph defines binary xᵢ for side
membership. Its cut is Σwᵢⱼ(xᵢ+xⱼ−2xᵢxⱼ), and the minimized QUBO is the negative cut. Under
sᵢ=2xᵢ−1, the Ising offset is −Σw/2 and pair coupling is wᵢⱼ/2. A separate exactly-one selection
adds A(Σxᵢ−1)² as a penalty.
maximize Σᵢvᵢxᵢ, subject to Σᵢwᵢxᵢ≤C, xᵢ∈{0,1}; Q(x)=−Σ_(i,j)wᵢⱼ(xᵢ+xⱼ−2xᵢxⱼ) (2)
4.B.2 Algorithms and experimental method
Greedy supplies a fast feasible lower bound; ILP, branch-and-bound and DP are exact on
these instances. Exhaustive enumeration independently checks all selections and QUBO/Ising
energies. Max-Cut uses the exact same hashed five-vertex fixture for Track C.
4.B.3 Results and interpretation
Greedy objectives were 160/220, 39/44 and 82/83 (gaps 27.27%, 11.36% and 1.20%). All
three exact methods matched exhaustive optima. Greedy runtimes were about 21–34 μs;
branch-and-bound about 40–100 μs; DP 0.30–1.46 ms; HiGHS ILP about 1.8–6.9 ms. The
five-vertex graph has seven weighted edges and exact maximum cut 8.0, with four equivalent
optimum bitstrings. A=5 was the smallest tested exactly-one penalty that made the ground
state feasible.

4.B.4 Correctness, limitations, and reproduction
For toy instances, solver startup dominates and greedy is fastest but can lose value. DP is
pseudo-polynomial in capacity; branch-and-bound has exponential worst-case complexity;
ILP supports richer constraints. The distinction between unconstrained Max-Cut (no penalty
needed) and the separate constrained example avoids assigning an irrelevant penalty to the
graph problem. Any additional class-specific discrete application remains syllabus-
dependent.

4.C Track C Quantum Optimization
4.C.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
C applies hybrid quantum-classical optimization to the exact Max-Cut instance from Track B,
then tests a small VQE Hamiltonian and a two-qubit parameterized quantum model. All
outputs are from local CPU simulation.
For graph edges E, the cost operator is H_C=−Σ_(i,j) wᵢⱼ(I−ZᵢZⱼ)/2, whose expectation is
negative cut value. QAOA alternates cost and mixer unitaries for depth p and optimizes
angles to minimize ⟨H_C⟩. VQE minimizes ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩. The parameter-shift derivative is
[f(θ+π/2)−f(θ−π/2)]/2. SPSA estimates a stochastic gradient using two objective evaluations
per iteration; quantum natural gradient preconditions by a circuit metric tensor.
H_C=−Σ_(i,j)wᵢⱼ(I−ZᵢZⱼ)/2; minimize_θ ⟨ψ(θ)|H_C|ψ(θ)⟩ (3)
4.C.2 Algorithms and experimental method
PennyLane statevector and mixed-state devices run QAOA, VQE, QNN, SPSA, parameter
shift, QNG, finite-shot and depolarizing-noise studies. Exhaustive classical enumeration
verifies all 32 Max-Cut basis energies. Circuit diagrams and quantum/classical reference
values are retained in the track result folder.
4.C.3 Results and interpretation
Mean expected cut rose from about 6.09 at p=1 to 6.87 at p=3. The best run had expected cut
7.2723, ratio 0.909, and 49.4% probability on exact optima; the most likely bitstring is
optimal. Twelve-shot repetitions reduced mean absolute estimator error from 0.0988 at 100
shots to 0.00954 at 10,000. Reference noise lowered expected cut from 7.2723 to 6.8040;
double noise lowered it to 6.4295. QNG reached the exact two-qubit VQE energy within
2.2×10⁻¹⁴ on three seeds. QNN and classical baseline both fit four training points perfectly,
but the classical model took about 0.0023 s versus about 0.85 s for QNN simulation. In the
embedding proxy, chain strengths 0.25, 1.0 and 3.0 left 100%, 25% and 0% of physical
ground states chain-broken.

4.D Track D Submodular, Parallel, and Distributed Optimization
4.D.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
D tests submodular maximum coverage, process parallel scheduling, three parallelization
forms, simulated federated averaging, and two-task loss balancing. The same deterministic
workloads are checked against serial or exhaustive references.
Coverage maximizes f(S)=|⋃_(j∈S)Aⱼ| subject to |S|≤k. For differentiable shared-model tasks,
FedAvg averages local models with client-size weights. The minimum-norm two-task
MGDA rule chooses α∈[0,1] minimizing ||αg₁+(1−α)g₂||²; GradNorm adapts positive task
weights to balance weighted gradient norms and relative training rates. Parallel efficiency is
Eₚ=T₁/(pTₚ), while speedup is T₁/Tₚ.
f(S)=|⋃_(j∈S)Aⱼ|, |S|≤k; speedup=T₁/Tₚ, efficciency=T₁/(pTₚ) (4)
4.D.2 Algorithms and experimental method
The set function was checked exhaustively for monotonicity and diminishing returns. Twelve
CPU-bound tasks were run serially, with contiguous static chunks, and through a process-
shared queue under macOS spawn. Work stealing was also demonstrated in a deterministic
event simulation. Separate data-, model- and pipeline-parallel calculations used
independently spawned processes and exact-output checks.
4.D.3 Results and interpretation
Greedy covered 7/10 elements while exhaustive optimum covered 8/10 (ratio 0.875). Static
scheduling produced 0.697× speedup at two workers and 0.846× at four. The real shared
queue achieved 1.065× median speedup at four workers, with 26.6% efficiency; repeats
included slowdowns. Data/model/pipeline parallel speedups were 0.00139, 0.000706 and
0.000810 because roughly 0.1 s process startup dwarfed 0.0001 s serial kernels. FedAvg with
one local step matched central GD exactly; at three local steps its 10-round loss was 0.33380
versus central 0.33345. Equal task weighting had mean loss 0.3977, GradNorm 0.4032, and
this MGDA run 1.2007 with a strong task imbalance.

4.E Track E Inference Request Batching and Scheduling
4.E.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
E studies inference-request batching using a reproducible discrete-event simulation of a
single server. Requests have seeded arrivals, token lengths, costs and SLO deadlines. No real
model, accelerator, energy use or production inference latency was measured.
Binary assignment variables map each request to exactly one ordered batch; all batches obey
request-count and token/padded-token capacities, arrival precedence, single-server non-
overlap, and bounded formation wait. Deadline violations use explicit nonnegative tardiness
slack. For batch b, modeled service is T_b=0.35+max(pᵢ)[1+0.22(n_b−1)]. The objective
combines mean latency, twice mean tardiness and 0.15 busy-time/request. This is a stated
simulation model, not measured hardware service.
T_b=0.35+max_(i∈b)(pᵢ)[1+0.22(|b|−1)] (5)
4.E.2 Algorithms and experimental method
Immediate FCFS is compared with bounded-window length-aware greedy and dynamic
programming that exactly solves a local count/token/urgency score over a 12-request
lookahead. Three workload sizes (24, 64, 128), two arrival rates, two token capacities and
three seeds share workload digests across methods. A six-request exhaustive ordered-
partition reference checks the local/global distinction.
4.E.3 Results and interpretation
At 128 requests and rate 1.2, capacity 512, FCFS mean/p95 latency were 12.911/24.390,
throughput 1.025 requests per simulated unit, makespan 124.986, utilization 0.994 and
deadline-miss rate 73.4%. Greedy reduced these to mean/p95 7.116/15.992, makespan
113.435 and 47.9% misses while throughput rose to 1.131; modeled utilization was 0.956. At
sparse rate 0.6, FCFS mean latency/wait were 3.186/1.016, compared with greedy
3.914/1.475 and DP 4.023/1.518, with similar throughput near 0.585. Greedy token capacity
384→512 improved loaded throughput 1.084→1.131, while miss rate shifted only
48.2%→47.9%.

4.F Track F Toy Language Compiler Optimization
4.F.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
F implements a small integer toy language with declarations, reassignment, arithmetic and
observable print statements. It is a lexer-parser-IR-interpreter pipeline, not a native-code
compiler.
The decision experiment chooses an ordered subset of compiler passes to minimize total
static IR instruction count subject to nominal optimization effort ≤8 and at most one use of
each pass. The full compiler separately applies four semantics-preserving transformations:
constant folding/propagation, common subexpression elimination, strength reduction for
positive powers of two, and dead-code elimination.

4.G Track G xv6 Priority Scheduling
4.G.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
G changes one feature in pinned MIT xv6-riscv: a priority scheduler with aging. The
measurement platform is a one-CPU RISC-V virtual machine under QEMU; it does not
characterize the macOS scheduler or physical RISC-V hardware.
The objective minimizes high-priority job turnaround while retaining work conservation and
low-priority completion. Priority is 0, 1 or 2, lower being higher; after eight runnable
scheduler rounds, waiting processes improve one effective level. The original scheduler scans
the process table in slot order and runs runnable processes as timer preemption returns them
to the scheduler.
effoective_priorityᵢ=max(0, base_priorityᵢ−⌊wait_roundsᵢ/8⌋) (7)
4.G.2 Algorithms and experimental method
The patch adds bounded priority setting and scheduler selection. Reads and writes of process
state, priority and waiting rounds remain under each p->lock. Only RUNNABLE processes
are candidates, selection changes exactly one to RUNNING before context switch, and only
the chosen process lock crosses swtch. Three identical workloads—CPU, timer-sleep I/O
surrogate and mixed—spawn six jobs behind a start barrier. Baseline and priority images
carry the same benchmark and syscall support.
4.G.3 Results and interpretation
For CPU-bound jobs, high-priority mean turnaround fell from 6.0 to 3.0 ticks; low-priority
mean shifted 6.89 to 6.78 and every job completed. Makespan changed 7.33→7.0 ticks and
throughput 0.821→0.857 jobs/tick. Jain's inverse-turnaround fairness fell 0.993→0.870,
exposing inequality. I/O-surrogate and mixed results were unchanged at 30 and 10 ticks
respectively. Pristine, support-baseline and optimized kernels each booted and passed full
usertests; 18 workload boots completed all 108 jobs.

4.H.1 Problem, objective, and formulation
H selects optional SQLite indexes under a measured 180-page storage budget. The synthetic

that index. Minimize C₀−Σbᵢxᵢ subject to Σsᵢxᵢ≤180 and xᵢ∈{0,1}. This is an additive selection

5 Cross Track Analysis
Several themes recur across the results. First, exact methods are most useful as references at
small scale: enumeration validates the knapsack solvers, Max-Cut Hamiltonian, submodular
coverage, compiler pass order, test-selection subset and tiny batch plan. At larger scales,
those methods become expensive, so the experiment reports a heuristic's verified gap or uses
a bounded local surrogate.
Second, optimization can shift cost instead of removing it. Track F reduced IR size but
compilation took longer. Track D's process overhead erased useful work on tiny kernels.
Track E's waiting window worsened sparse-load latency. Track J gained candidate coverage
at nearly identical end-to-end short-suite runtime because process startup dominated. Track G
traded response equality for faster high-priority CPU completion. These are practical costs of
each decision rule, not defects to conceal.
Third, resource metrics need context. High utilization in Track E accompanied a high miss
rate under overload. Track H's latency reduction depended on a read-heavy query mix and
180-page storage allowance. Track A's objective values are comparable only where the

7 Conclusion
This project implemented and measured ten separate optimization studies with a shared,
reproducible Python repository. The strongest gains were domain-specific: exact
combinatorial solvers confirmed optima on small instances; load-aware inference batching
improved the simulated overloaded workload; priority scheduling reduced high-priority CPU
turnaround in xv6; SQLite's selected indexes materially reduced the measured read workload;
and greedy test selection captured more candidate line coverage within the same profiled
budget. Other experiments produced negative or mixed outcomes, including process
overhead, sparse batching delay, longer compiler build time and fairness loss under priority
scheduling.
The evidence supports only the stated scale and environment. It does not establish production
performance, native compiler speedups, real LLM inference improvements or quantum
advantage. The report also remains conditional on the exact Chapter 6 syllabus and a real
two-tool comparison. Completing those two academic checks is necessary before describing
assignment coverage as fully closed.
The central conclusion is methodological: a credible optimization result requires an explicit
objective, fair baseline, constraint checks, independent correctness evidence and an honest
account of overhead. Several methods increased quality only under particular load or cost
assumptions; their performance reversed when those assumptions changed.
